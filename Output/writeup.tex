\section*{Task 1: Data Preparation and Surprise Measure Selection}
%==============================================================================

\subsection*{(a) Exploration of Surprise Measures}

The USMPD contains multiple surprise measures constructed from federal funds futures around FOMC announcements: MP1 (target rate surprise from current-month contracts), MP2 (path surprise from 3-month-ahead contracts), ED1--ED4 (Eurodollar-based surprises), and principal component measures. I focus on MP1 and STMT (statement surprise), where STMT is a principal component that aggregates information across short-rate instruments, capturing both target and forward-guidance news.

\subsection*{(b) Primary Measure: STMT}

I use STMT as my baseline surprise measure for three reasons: (1) it summarizes both target rate changes and forward guidance in a single interpretable series; (2) it correlates highly with both MP1 ($\rho = 0.89$) and MP2 ($\rho = 0.73$), capturing multiple dimensions of monetary news; (3) it explains a larger share of Treasury yield variation than MP1 alone ($R^2 = 0.27$ vs.\ 0.14 for the 2-year yield). MP1 serves as a robustness measure focusing narrowly on target rate surprises.

\subsection*{(c) Data Merge}

The sample covers 274 FOMC announcements from January 1994 to December 2024. I merge surprises with: (i) daily spot exchange rates for 8 currencies (AUD, CAD, CHF, EUR, GBP, JPY, MXN, NOK); (ii) Treasury par yields (2Y, 5Y, 10Y) from the U.S. Department of the Treasury; (iii) breakeven inflation rates (5Y, 10Y) from FRED. Exchange rate returns are defined as $r_{i,t} = -\Delta\log(e_{i,t})$ where $e$ is foreign currency per USD, so positive values indicate foreign appreciation. NFA/GDP data come from \citet{lane2018external}, matched using lagged annual values to avoid reverse causality.

\subsection*{(d) Summary Statistics}

Table~\ref{tab:summary} presents summary statistics. Both surprise measures have means near zero (consistent with market efficiency) with fat tails reflecting crisis-period announcements. STMT has standard deviation 3.7 bps, with range $[-16.9, +12.7]$ bps. Cross-country NFA/GDP varies substantially: Switzerland (+142\% GDP) and Norway (+68\%) are large creditors; Australia ($-52\%$) and Mexico ($-39\%$) are debtors.

\section*{Task 2: Asset Price Responses to Monetary Policy}
%==============================================================================

I estimate currency- and asset-specific regressions:
\begin{equation}
\Delta y_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i \cdot \text{Surprise}_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}
\end{equation}
with heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.

\subsection*{Treasury Yields}

Table~\ref{tab:treasury} reports Treasury yield responses. A 1 bp STMT surprise raises the 2-year yield by 1.06 bps ($t = 10.4$, $R^2 = 0.27$), with effects declining at longer maturities (0.68 bps for 10Y). The strong, significant responses validate the identification strategy: these surprises capture meaningful monetary policy news that moves the yield curve. Figure~\ref{fig:validation} visualizes this relationship.

\subsection*{Breakeven Inflation}

Breakeven responses (Table~\ref{tab:treasury}) are smaller in magnitude and less precisely estimated: 0.21 bps for 5Y ($R^2 = 0.02$), 0.10 bps for 10Y ($R^2 = 0.01$). This suggests monetary surprises move real rates more than inflation expectations, consistent with standard New Keynesian transmission.

\subsection*{Exchange Rates}

Table~\ref{tab:fx} reports exchange rate responses. Coefficients are heterogeneous across currencies (ranging from $-1.6$ bps for JPY to $+0.8$ bps for MXN per 1 bp shock) and imprecisely estimated---none is individually significant at conventional levels. $R^2$ values are uniformly below 1\%, indicating daily FX returns are dominated by non-monetary factors. Figure~\ref{fig:fx_coef} visualizes the coefficient heterogeneity.

\textbf{Interpretation}: The contrast between strong yield responses and noisy FX responses suggests limited power in a daily event-study design for detecting cross-country heterogeneity. This motivates the panel approach in Part 3, which pools information across currencies.

\section*{Task 3: The Role of External Positions}
%==============================================================================

I estimate a panel regression testing whether NFA/GDP moderates exchange rate responses:
\begin{equation}
\Delta e_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Surprise}_t + \beta_2 \cdot (\text{Surprise}_t \times \text{NFA}_{i,t-1}) + \gamma \cdot \text{NFA}_{i,t-1} + \varepsilon_{i,t}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Standard Error Choice}

Because the monetary surprise is identical across currencies on each FOMC date, I cluster standard errors by announcement date following Petersen (2009). This is the appropriate clustering dimension when the regressor of interest varies only at the time level. Clustering by country would understate uncertainty since it ignores the common-shock structure. Table~\ref{tab:panel} also reports two-way clustered and country-clustered specifications for robustness.

\subsection*{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:panel} presents the results. The key finding is that the interaction coefficient $\hat{\beta}_2 = -0.005$ (SE $= 0.007$, $p = 0.48$) is statistically indistinguishable from zero. The Bruno--Shin balance-sheet hypothesis predicts $\beta_2 > 0$ (creditor currencies depreciate less on hawkish surprises). The point estimate is actually negative, though imprecision precludes strong conclusions.

Figure~\ref{fig:marginal} shows the marginal effect of STMT across the NFA distribution. The 95\% confidence interval includes zero throughout, and the slope is essentially flat.

\textbf{Economic interpretation}: The data do not support NFA/GDP as a sufficient statistic for cross-country FX sensitivity at daily frequency. This null result is informative: the 95\% CI rules out interaction effects larger than $\pm 0.02$, which would correspond to economically meaningful differential responses across debtor and creditor nations.

\subsection*{Challenges and Limitations}

Several factors may explain the null result:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Daily frequency noise}: Daily FX returns are dominated by non-monetary factors, limiting statistical power.
    \item \textbf{Net vs.\ gross exposure}: Net NFA may not capture gross currency mismatches that drive balance-sheet effects.
    \item \textbf{Cross-sectional power}: With only 8 currencies, detecting moderate interaction effects requires large effect sizes; a power analysis suggests $\sim$30 currencies would be needed.
    \item \textbf{Sample composition}: The sample excludes emerging markets with higher dollar debt shares where balance-sheet vulnerabilities may be more pronounced.
\end{itemize}

%==============================================================================
\section*{Task 4: Extension --- Time Variation in the Balance-Sheet Channel}
%==============================================================================

\subsection*{Hypothesis}

If the NFA channel operates through dollar funding stress, it should strengthen after the 2008 global financial crisis, when dollar liquidity became central to global finance. I test this by adding a triple interaction:
\begin{align}
\Delta e_{i,t} &= \alpha_i + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Surprise}_t + \beta_2 \cdot (\text{Surprise}_t \times \text{NFA}) \nonumber \\
&\quad + \beta_3 \cdot (\text{Surprise}_t \times \text{NFA} \times \text{Post2008}) + \text{controls} + \varepsilon_{i,t}
\end{align}
where Post2008 = 1 for dates after September 15, 2008.\footnote{The Lehman Brothers bankruptcy on this date is a standard structural break in the global finance literature; see \citet{bruno2015cross}.}

\subsection*{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:extension} presents the results. The triple interaction $\hat{\beta}_3 = 0.022$ (SE $= 0.015$, $p = 0.15$) is positive, in the direction predicted by theory, but not statistically significant. For STMT, the NFA slope shifts from $-0.015$ (pre-GFC) to $+0.007$ (post-GFC). This directional movement is toward the Bruno--Shin prediction ($\beta_2 > 0$), but the change is statistically imprecise.

I also test whether the channel operates during high-volatility episodes (VIX $>$ 75th percentile). The stress interaction is similarly insignificant ($p = 0.20$), providing no robust evidence of amplification during market stress.

\subsection*{Power Analysis}

Given the null results, I assess what effect sizes the data can rule out. The standard error on $\beta_2$ implies a minimum detectable effect (at 80\% power) of approximately $|0.007| \times 2.8 \approx 0.02$. With NFA SD $\approx 70$ percentage points, this corresponds to differential FX responses of $\sim$1.4 percentage points per 10 bp shock across extreme debtors and creditors---about 20\% of daily FX volatility. Thus, the null result bounds the channel's magnitude at practically relevant levels.

%==============================================================================
\section*{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================

U.S. Treasury yields respond strongly to monetary policy surprises, validating the high-frequency identification strategy. Exchange rate responses are noisy and heterogeneous at daily frequency, with $R^2 < 1\%$. Net foreign asset positions do not robustly mediate FX responses---neither unconditionally, post-2008, nor during high-volatility episodes. These null results do not reject the theoretical relevance of balance-sheet channels; rather, they indicate that within a daily-frequency event-study design using 8 advanced-economy currencies, NFA/GDP is not a sufficient statistic for currency exposure to U.S. monetary shocks. Extensions using gross position measures or larger cross-sections of emerging-market currencies may yield sharper identification.
